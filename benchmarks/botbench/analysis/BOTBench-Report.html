Sitrec · BOT Bench · Round 1 · 2026-07-22

How well can bearings-only tracking recover a target — and can we tell when it can't?

A synthetic benchmark of Sitrec's line-of-sight solvers: 855 generated scenarios, 6,848 solver runs, five questions, and a co-designed audit trail with Codex. Everything below runs locally in about 40 seconds.

1What this is

Sitrec's traverse analysis reconstructs a 3D trajectory from bearings only — a sequence of sightlines from a moving camera, with no range information. This is the classic bearings-only tracking (BOT) problem, and it sits under every "what was that object" analysis: the same sightlines are usually consistent with a slow close object, a fast far one, and many things between.

BOT Bench measures, with known ground truth, how well the fast solvers actually do across the realistic problem space: a fixed-wing sensor aircraft with a very narrow field of view (<1°), tracking balloons, birds, aircraft, Venus, and deliberately impossible "anomalous" targets, under varying flight geometry, wind, clip length, and pointing error — including a simulated human operator's wobble. It was co-designed and audited in an adversarial loop with a second model (Codex), which caught real errors before they reached these pages (§6).

2What was built

A deterministic scenario generator, a solver harness, and an analysis stack — pure math, no browser, seeded end to end so every number regenerates bit-identically.

The scenario space covers six platform paths (orbits around a point, orbits around a misjudged position at 0.5×/1×/2× the true range, a constant-bank curve, straight cruise, and two S-weaves), nine target families (rising and neutral party balloons, weather balloons, high-altitude balloons at 18–20 km, a tethered aerostat, a meandering bird, cruising and turning aircraft, ephemeris-true Venus, and parameterized anomalies with matched 2.5 g ordinary-maneuver controls), four wind models, clip lengths from 5 to 120 s, and three pointing-error modes — clean, white Gaussian, and a seeded human-operator wobble model whose paired white twin is rescaled to the exact same realized noise power.

3How recovery is scored

Two numbers are always reported separately, because they disagree in exactly the cases that matter: the LOS residual (how closely the answer threads the sightlines) and the truth error (how far the answer is from where the object really was). A fit can hug the sightlines better while being kilometres more wrong — the benchmark measured residual improving while truth error tripled, and that divergence is a headline result, not a nuisance.

The key failure mode is collapse: with weak geometry, the sensor's own flight path is a perfect zero-residual solution, so free solvers put the "target" on the camera. Collapsed answers are counted (as non-detections, flagged per run), never dropped — an earlier version silently dropped them and inflated results (§6).

4.4  Anomalies are only visible to rigid models

Six impossible events (100 g pulses, instant 150 m/s velocity steps, 1-second 180° reversals) were each paired with an ordinary 2.5 g maneuver sharing the same onset, direction, and pointing-error realization. Can a solver's residual stream tell them apart, event-blind?

4.6  Choosing the solver from observables alone

The practical question: given only what an analyst can actually see — the sightlines, the cheap solvers' residuals, the conditioning score, never the truth — can a rule pick the right solver? Rules were trained on 504 scenarios and evaluated on 124 held-out ones (leakage-checked by an independent assertion; all metrics group-weighted).

Geometry gives the test set a 58.9% catastrophic floor: in most scenarios no available solver gets within 10% of true range, no matter how it's chosen. What a selection rule controls is the avoidable catastrophe above that floor:

Within-regime detail. Where geometry permits recovery at all, the rule scores 0.898 accuracy with 0% catastrophe. In structurally collapsed scenarios every action is catastrophic (floor 100%) — there the right output is not a solver choice but an abstention, and the conditioning score from Fig. 3 is precisely the abstention signal. Restricting answers by that score trades coverage for honesty; it does not create safety by itself.

5Limitations

Transfer across platform families is imperfect. A rule trained with no straight-flight scenarios degrades badly on them (accuracy 0.56, catastrophe above floor); trained with no curves, it loses ~25 points on curves. The selection rule must see degenerate geometry in training. The noise-color finding is a leading interpretation — five pairs per cell, overlapping spreads. Direction-regime (Venus) conclusions rest on the sentinel pool; too few Venus groups reach the test split. The classifier evaluation is audit-corrected rather than freshly preregistered: the test partition was seen before the final grouping repair (models and hyperparameters were never tuned on it). Flat-terrain proxy: site "terrain" is an altitude offset, not a mesh; real-terrain interaction is a round-2, in-app question. Wobble parameters are plausible, not calibrated to real operator data.

6How the numbers were kept honest

Every stage ran through an adversarial review loop with a second model (Codex), which had full code access and independently recomputed results. Four of its catches materially changed published numbers:

  • Silent collapse-dropping inflated anomaly detection. Runs where the Kalman smoother collapsed exactly onto the sensor produced undefined residuals and fell out of the AUC pool — precisely the most-failed runs. CV's anomaly AUC fell from 0.74 to 0.55 once collapsed runs were counted as non-detections.
  • Train/test leakage through deterministic twins. Gust-free scenarios are seed-independent, so identical truth appeared on both sides of the classifier split (twice, two different ways) until grouping was rebuilt from a canonical truth-content key and enforced by an independent assertion.
  • A prevalence trap in the regime detector. With 87% of scenarios CV-unrecoverable, an accuracy-optimized threshold silently matched "always say unrecoverable"; the reported operating point is now Youden-optimal.
  • A bootstrap that cancelled itself. Reweighting resampled clusters undid the resampling; confidence intervals were recomputed with fixed weights.

The full negotiation, contract, and amendment history live in benchmarks/botbench/PLAN.md.

8Round 2

Deferred by design, recorded in the plan: the slow physics solvers (balloon, drone, fixed-wing differential evolution) on the interesting cells; UWYO/GFS sounding wind fixtures; burst/parachute and superpressure balloon truths; real terrain in-app; a bridge that emits any scenario as a loadable Sitrec sitch for visual inspection; and operator-wobble parameters calibrated against real tracking footage. 9Glossary

Plain-language definitions of the terms this report leans on, in the sense they're used here.

Bearings-only tracking (BOT)
Working out where something is using only the directions it was seen in over time — never how far away it was. The central difficulty: a nearby slow object and a distant fast one can look identical.
Line of sight (LOS) / sightline
One camera-to-object direction at one moment: "from here, it was that way." A clip yields one sightline per video frame.
Parallax
The apparent shift of an object against its background when the viewer moves sideways. The only source of distance information in bearings-only data — no sideways motion, no range.
Range / slant range
Distance from the camera to the object. "Slant" means the straight-line distance, including the height difference, not just the map distance.
Solver / fit
An algorithm that proposes a 3D path consistent with the sightlines, under some assumption about how the object moves (straight line, smooth curve, drifting balloon…). "Fit" is both the verb and the resulting path.
Constant velocity (CV)
The simplest motion assumption: straight line, steady speed. Fast, and correct surprisingly often — when the geometry gives it a chance.
Kalman smoother
A solver that walks through the clip frame by frame, balancing "keep moving as before" against "follow the measurements," then repeats the pass backwards so every point benefits from the whole clip.
Process noise
The Kalman smoother's flexibility dial: low values assume the object flies very steadily (smooth but stiff), high values let it maneuver (responsive but noisier).
Monte Carlo fit
A solver that tries many randomized guesses and keeps the best. Here the randomness comes from a fixed seed, so the same inputs always give the same answer.
Fixed-point fit
The hypothesis that the object never moved at all — one point in space that best explains every sightline. Rigid, humble, and hard to beat when the object really is nearly stationary.
Fixed-direction fit
The hypothesis that the object is effectively infinitely far away (a star, a planet), so every sightline should point the same way. The natural Venus detector.
Residual
How closely a proposed path threads the sightlines, in degrees. Small residual means "consistent with what was seen" — it does not mean the path is where the object was.
Truth error
How far the proposed path is from where the (synthetic) object actually was. Only measurable in a benchmark, where the truth is known — which is the point of building one.
Collapse
The classic failure: with weak geometry, the mathematically best "object path" is the camera's own flight path, so the solver puts the object metres from the lens. The residual still looks excellent.
Conditioning (rcond)
A single number computed from the sightlines alone that grades whether the geometry can support range recovery for the straight-line solver family. Low values mean high collapse risk. A warning gauge, not a verdict.
Field of view (FOV)
The angular width of what the camera sees. Under 1° here — a very powerful zoom, roughly a soda straw held at arm's length.
Operator wobble
Simulated human tracking error: the aim drifts off the target, the operator notices, and corrects — producing errors that persist over time rather than jumping randomly frame to frame.
White noise / matched-power noise
Random pointing error that is independent every frame. "Matched-power" means a white-noise twin scaled to exactly the same total error energy as its wobble counterpart, so only the error's character differs.
Oracle
The impossible ideal used as a yardstick: the choice you'd make if you already knew the truth. "Oracle floor" is the failure rate even the ideal chooser can't avoid — failure the geometry itself imposes.
Catastrophic / excess catastrophe
A result is catastrophic here when the recovered range is off by more than 10%. Excess catastrophe is how much a decision rule adds above the oracle floor — the avoidable part.
Regret
The extra error a rule's chosen solver incurs compared with the best solver available for that scenario. Zero regret means the pick cost nothing, even if the scenario itself was hopeless.
ROC AUC
A 0-to-1 score for a detector: the probability it ranks a true case above a false one. 0.5 is a coin flip; 0.9 is genuinely useful.
Sentinel scenarios
A small set of scenarios locked away from all rule-training and threshold-picking, used only to check that conclusions survive on data nothing was tuned to.
Seeded / deterministic
All "randomness" comes from fixed starting numbers (seeds), so every run of the benchmark reproduces the same 855 scenarios and the same results, bit for bit.
Ephemeris
The astronomical tables giving where a celestial body (here, Venus) actually was in the sky at a given time and place — the benchmark uses the real thing, not a stand-in.
