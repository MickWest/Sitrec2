A Ground-Truth Benchmark of Bearings-Only Trajectory Recovery for Narrow-Field Airborne Sensors

Mick West · Sitrec project (github.com/MickWest/Sitrec2)

Draft prepared with an AI-assisted adversarial analysis loop (two independent models cross-auditing design, code, and results); all findings reproduce deterministically from the cited open-source harness.

DRAFT v0.7 for author review (emerging-threats capability section added and audited) — every quantitative and operational claim verified against the benchmark records through the adversarial audit loop; the capability section carries a final estimator-discipline pass (α* the family minimum vs α̂ the reported optimizer upper-bound estimate; the geometry test named a pairwise separability screen, distinct from forced-exceedance attributability) now under re-verification. Abstract

Video from narrow-field airborne electro-optical sensors records directions, not positions: every disputed object trajectory must be reconstructed from bearings alone, and the same sightlines are typically consistent with a slow nearby object, a fast distant one, and a continuum between. We present a deterministic, ground-truth benchmark for this reconstruction problem — 855 synthetic scenarios spanning surveillance-relevant platform geometries (orbits, arcs, cruise, weaves), target classes (balloons, birds, aircraft, celestial objects, and kinematically impossible controls), wind models, clip lengths of 5–120 s, and pointing-error models including simulated human-operator tracking wobble — and evaluate fourteen solver configurations spanning four method families against known truth — from constant-velocity least squares and a Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoother to physically constrained balloon, multirotor, and fixed-wing model fits (the physics and spline methods on a 140-cell subset). Four results are operationally significant: (1) sensor geometry and observation time dominate everything else — under a non-maneuvering sensor with realistic (0.03°) pointing noise the fully swept linear estimators recovered no range at any clip length (a rigid stationary-point hypothesis stayed within 3–12% on its near-stationary targets), while a 60-second orbit at 2 km range recovered it to under one percent (the same orbit at 20 km still failed); (2) a single conditioning statistic computed from the sightlines alone grades, before any fitting, the risk that the linear estimator family will collapse onto the sensor; (3) physically constrained model fits recover useful range in geometries where the tested linear estimators fail, at the cost of reduced accuracy where geometry is strong; and (4) a solver-selection rule using only observable features eliminates essentially all avoidable gross range error on held-out scenarios (0.0 added percentage points above the 58.9% floor that geometry imposes on the benchmarked estimator set, versus +31.2 for always using the standard estimator). The benchmark also functioned as a defect-discovery instrument for the analysis tool itself, surfacing and confirming two production issues in deployed fitting code. For the emerging-threats question — surfacing an adversary capability a modest margin outside a known envelope, or a coherent new capability class — we frame anomalous motion as one endpoint of a capability continuum, characterize a geometry-dependent separability screen for when an exceedance is even distinguishable from its in-envelope counterpart, and give a constrained-feasibility statistic (α*, the minimum envelope scale a searched family of bearings-consistent trajectories requires; reported as an optimizer upper-bound estimate α̂) validated as a measurement. We find that signature separability and forced exceedance are distinct thresholds, and we deliberately make no quantitative detection-rate claim, as that awaits threshold calibration. We state limitations plainly: results are synthetic, the operator-wobble model is uncalibrated, and terrain is a flat-datum proxy.

1The operational problem

Assessments of unidentified objects recorded by airborne sensors almost always reduce to a single estimation problem: given a time series of camera positions and pointing directions, what trajectories are consistent with the recording? This is the classic bearings-only target motion analysis (TMA) problem [1], transplanted from towed-array sonar to gimballed electro-optical pods with fields of view under one degree.

The difficulty is structural, not technological. A bearing measurement constrains the target to a ray; range along that ray is only determined by parallax — the sensor must move around the sightlines, not merely along them. When it does not, the data admit a family of mutually contradictory reconstructions: the slow close drifter and the fast distant maneuverer produce the same pixels, and disagreements over particular recordings are frequently disagreements over which member of that family to prefer — often without any statement of whether the data can distinguish them at all.

An analyst therefore needs answers to four questions before any interpretation is defensible:

  • Does this geometry support range recovery at all?
  • Which reconstruction method is appropriate, and how would one know without access to truth?
  • What does goodness-of-fit actually prove? (Short answer: far less than intuition suggests.)
  • Would a genuinely anomalous trajectory even be visible through these methods, or would they smooth it away?

This paper answers those questions with measured error rates rather than argument, using a ground-truth benchmark built around the open-source Sitrec reconstruction tool. The benchmark's scenario families were chosen to span the commonly proposed candidate explanations for narrow-field airborne recordings (a coverage choice, not a prevalence-weighted population): wind-borne balloons of several types, birds, conventional aircraft, celestial objects, and — as controls — trajectories containing deliberately impossible kinematic events.

2Methods under test

Fourteen solver configurations were evaluated, in four families (the linear family includes one smoother at three tunings; the physics and ray-constrained methods ran on a 140-cell round-2 subset):

  • Linear kinematic fits. Constant-velocity (CV) and constant-acceleration least squares over perpendicular ray distance; a six-state Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoother [2] with the CV process model at three process-noise settings; seeded Monte Carlo and deterministic alternating polynomial fits. These are fast (milliseconds) and assumption-light.
  • Rigid geometric hypotheses. A single stationary point best explaining all sightlines, and a fixed direction (the at-infinity hypothesis appropriate to celestial objects).
  • Ray-constrained smoothness methods. A minimum-acceleration spline over range profiles (with a soft speed prior for degenerate geometry) and a minimum-speed solve (the slowest object consistent with the sightlines).
  • Physically constrained model fits. Forward-integrated dynamics fitted by differential evolution with local polish: a wind-driven balloon model, a hover-capable multirotor model, and a constant-airspeed fixed-wing model.

All methods consume the same sightlines — sensor positions and unit pointing vectors per frame; the ray-constrained spline methods and the fixed-wing fit additionally receive the scenario's wind series (as in the deployed tool), while the balloon and multirotor fits solve for wind themselves. The Monte Carlo configuration ran on eight sentinel scenarios only. Finite-track results are scored by angular residual against the sightlines (how well the answer explains the data) and three-dimensional separation from truth (whether the answer is right); celestial cases are scored by apparent-direction error. Residual and truth error are reported separately throughout, because the benchmark repeatedly measures them disagreeing.

Three design rules were enforced from the outset. First, no inverse crime: truth generators do not live inside any solver's model class (balloon truth comes from an independent integrator with gusts and layered veer no fitted model can represent), and sightlines carry noise — except in cells deliberately constructed as oracle-compatible controls (constant-velocity aircraft, constant-wind floaters, clean observation), which are labeled as controls and flagged wherever they inform a conclusion (as in §4.6's strong-geometry comparison, whose target is deliberately CV-representable). Second, fit quality and truth error are never conflated: the benchmark's own data contain cases where a method's residual improves while its truth error grows severalfold. Third, adversarial audit: every phase — design, implementation, statistics — was independently reviewed by a second AI system with full code access, which re-derived results before they were accepted. This process materially changed published numbers: it caught a selection bias that had inflated an anomaly-detection score from 0.55 to 0.74, two train/test leakage paths in the selection-rule evaluation, an invalid bootstrap, and a prevalence artifact in a detector threshold. We describe this because the corrected findings are only as credible as the process that attacked them.

The operational reading: under realistic pointing noise, a non-maneuvering sensor gives the swept linear estimators no range at any clip length (noiseless controls can land arbitrarily: a degenerate system breaks its ties unpredictably) (one exception in the record: the sentinel-only Monte Carlo configuration recovered a single straight-flight case to 4.4% — an isolated result whose mechanism we did not investigate) — the sensor's own trajectory satisfies the estimator's least-squares objective exactly, and the estimator returns it as confidently as any genuine solution — the failure is invisible to the method itself. An orbiting sensor transitions from useless (5 s of arc) to sub-percent recovery (60 s) as subtended arc accumulates; distance pushes the transition later. This is the Nardone–Aidala observability requirement [1] made quantitative for this sensor class. Notably, the humble stationary-point hypothesis scored 3–12% error across every cell of this table (median per cell; the target drifts only slowly, so a fixed point is nearly correct) — a rigid, nearly correct model is robust precisely where flexible ones collapse.

4.2  Failure is predictable before fitting

The reciprocal condition number of the (column-equilibrated, time-normalized) constant-velocity design system — computable from the sightlines alone, before any fitting — grades collapse risk sharply. In the geometry/duration block, the CV collapse rate was 82% in the log10(rcond) ≈ −3 bin, 72% at ≈ −2.5, and 0% at ≈ −2 and above. A formally trained binary detector on this one feature (threshold selected by Youden's J on training data at −2.46; weighted ROC-AUC 0.79) reached zero false positives on the held-out sentinel pool. Two disciplined caveats: the statistic speaks only for the linear estimator family — rigid and physically constrained methods can succeed where it warns — and it is one-way: good conditioning is never proof the recovered range is right. These findings have since been implemented in the analysis tool: each live linear-family fit now displays its conditioning and collapse state rather than silently publishing a degenerate track.

4.3  Human-like tracking error is worse than its power suggests

The benchmark's simulated operator error is autocorrelated — aim drifts, is noticed, and is corrected — where analysis conventionally assumes white noise. (Real-operator calibration is future work; the mechanism, not the magnitudes, is the claim here.) With wobble and white twins matched to identical realized error power in the recoverable regime: constant velocity degraded 4.7× under wobble relative to white on the orbit cells and 3.3× on the arc cells; the default-tuned smoother 2.8× and 1.9×; a soft-tuned smoother (process noise 10× higher) was essentially immune (0.9× and 0.4×) — at the price of a several-times-worse baseline against the same matched-power white noise (6.5 m vs CV's 1.0 m and the default smoother's 2.2 m). Noise color, not just noise power, should set filter tuning, and hand-tracked or autotracker-derived pointing merits a process-noise sensitivity sweep as standard practice. (Five pairs per cell, overlapping spreads — a leading interpretation, not settled law; and the wobble model itself is behaviorally plausible but not yet calibrated against measured operator tracking error.)

The discriminating signal is the pattern of residuals across a panel of model classes, not any single fit — though this is event-dependent (a pure velocity step along the line of sight barely registers) and was measured on clean sightlines; noise-robustness testing remains open.

4.5  Celestial objects present a distinctive signature

On true-ephemeris Venus scenarios the at-infinity hypothesis matched the apparent direction to 0.016°. The moving-model estimators (constant velocity, constant acceleration, the smoother) collapsed onto the camera — fitted ranges of metres, direction errors of 74–90°, with a median per-scenario 33–49% of frames behind the sensor (pooled frame rates run higher, 53–76%; 83–100% of scenarios contain behind-sensor frames) — and the order-2 polynomial collapsed similarly (median range 4 m, 1.7° direction error). The stationary-point hypothesis behaved differently, as it should for a target at infinity: it matched the direction well (0.09°) at an arbitrary large distance (~70 km), since any sufficiently distant fixed point fits near-parallel sightlines. The celestial signature is therefore a pattern: unanimous moving-model collapse, a stationary point at large but unstable range, and one excellent at-infinity fit — a pattern suitable for an automated screen (observed on twelve scenarios; no such screen was implemented or evaluated here).

4.6  Physical constraints rescue range where geometry fails — and cost accuracy where it doesn't

The physically constrained fits were evaluated on 140 cells spanning all regimes (420 physics-model runs, alongside 280 spline and 280 linear-baseline runs; 980 total). Two complementary results:

  • In the geometry/duration block — including straight-flight sensors where every linear fit collapses to 100% error — the balloon model recovered range to a median 9% and the multirotor model to 4% (the multirotor figure is partially bound-assisted at the 20 km tier, where its search cap coincides with true range). Physical priors (a balloon must ride the wind; a drone obeys an envelope) supply the constraint that geometry does not.
  • Where geometry is strong, the ordering reverses: on recoverable balloon cells the linear fits achieved 0.05–0.3% error against the balloon model's 1.1–2.5% and the multirotor's 0.1–1.9% — flexibility, regularization, and stochastic search have a cost when parallax already pins the answer.
Two-regime rule. Strong geometry → prefer simple kinematic estimators, with the conditioning check used as a warning gauge (a "good" reading is never proof of recovered range). Weak geometry → only physically constrained hypotheses (and rigid geometric ones) carry information; the linear fits return artifacts with excellent-looking objective values.

The balloon model's inferred base wind agreed with truth to ~1 m/s in the recoverable-geometry cells (median 2.3 m/s across all sixty balloon cells), degrading to ~5.9 m/s — with a documented calm-wind prior pull toward zero — in the weak-geometry block. This quantifies when "the fitted wind matches the weather data" constitutes evidence, with one label of honesty: the metric covers the model's base-wind parameters only, not its full time-varying wind representation.

4.7  Method selection from observables alone

Finally, the practical question: can a rule choose the right method using only what an analyst can actually observe — residuals of cheap fits, the conditioning statistic, coverage and stability diagnostics — never the truth? On a leakage-controlled held-out test set, 58.9% (group-weighted) of scenarios defeated every method in the benchmarked selection set (the linear family plus the stationary point): no choice within that set gets within 10% of true range. This is an action-set-specific floor, not proof that range is fundamentally unknowable there — §4.6 shows physically constrained methods recovering useful range in some of the same geometries. Two lessons stand out. Choosing whichever method best hugs the sightlines is nearly as bad as never adapting at all — residual quality is not evidence of truth. And in structurally collapsed scenarios every choice within this action set is catastrophic; the honest output there is to declare range indeterminate for unconstrained methods and fall back to the physically constrained panel of §4.6. The conditioning statistic is a candidate trigger for that declaration — though thresholding it was measured to trade coverage for honesty without by itself improving safety, so it flags indeterminacy rather than guaranteeing anything. Caveats recorded: the rule's cross-geometry transfer is imperfect (a rule trained without straight-flight scenarios degrades on them), the evaluation is audit-corrected rather than freshly preregistered, and celestial-regime conclusions rest on a small sentinel pool.

4.8  Surfacing emerging capability, not just excluding anomalies

An emerging-threats reading of §4.4 inverts its question. There, physically impossible maneuvers were controls — the far endpoint of a spectrum, used to check that the residual machinery reacts to gross incompatibility. The operationally harder problem is the middle of that spectrum: an adversary system running a modest margin outside a known performance envelope (say 20% over a catalogued top speed or climb rate), or a physically coherent capability combination that no catalogued class holds (fighter-class sustained g at drone speeds; a hover-to-dash transition; extreme sustained climb). We treat the whole range as one continuum — in-envelope → marginal exceedance → unrepresented-but-coherent technology → gross/impossible — and ask, for each rung, whether bearings-only data can surface it.

Two questions, kept separate. First, pairwise separability: does an exceeded signature look different, in the sightlines, from its matched in-envelope (λ=1) counterpart? (This is a pairwise test against one counterfactual — not distinguishability from every admissible alternative, which is the harder attributability question below.) This is geometry, not algorithm — we measure the clean-truth angular separation between a signature flown at scale λ and the same signature at λ=1, and compare it to the amplitude of the (uncalibrated) operator-wobble model (~0.15°) as a rough screening scale, not an empirically established detection threshold. At the recoverable geometry (a 60-second orbit at 2 km) a 20% multirotor speed or climb excess separates well clear of that scale (1.05° and 0.51° respectively); at weak geometry (a 15-second orbit at 20 km) the same small-platform excess is far smaller (0.04° and 0.03°) — nonzero in clean data, but of the same order as the wobble-model amplitude, so whether it would survive realistic pointing error is untested here. A larger platform's speed signature (fixed-wing) remains well separated even there (0.25° at +20%). This is a geometry- and platform-dependent separability screen — when an exceeded signature is even distinguishable in principle from its in-envelope counterpart. It is not a minimum-detectable-exceedance estimate (only three scales, λ = {1, 1.2, 2}, were sampled) and, uncalibrated against noise, it is not by itself grounds for a "nothing unusual" conclusion.

Measuring exceedance is a constrained-feasibility question, not a recovery. A negative result governs the method. Because bearings only constrain the object to the rays — X(t)=S(t)+λ(t)·D(t) is consistent with the data for any positive range profile λ(t) — there is no prior-free way to recover the range, and "how fast was it going" is ill-posed. The well-posed version treats range as a nuisance parameter and asks an existence question: over a searched family of bandwidth-limited on-ray range profiles consistent with the sightlines, what is the minimum envelope scale α* that any of them requires? If some searched trajectory stays within the catalogued envelope (α* ≤ 1), exceedance is not forced within that family — an in-envelope explanation exists, and the honest output is abstention (also the correct output at weak geometry, where a slower, nearer in-envelope reading always exists). The statistic uses hard catalogue limits over the whole trajectory — horizontal speed, signed vertical rate, load factor — with no plausibility preference (a cruise-speed or calm-wind prior would bias toward the mundane reading and hide real exceedance), and its only regularization is the bandwidth limit implied by the spline knot spacing. Two honest qualifications bound the claim. The search is over a finite-dimensional family (a continuous log-range B-spline of bounded knot count, 3-second bandwidth, a range floor), not literally every consistent trajectory; and the solver returns an optimizer estimate α̂ that is an upper bound on that family's true minimum α*. So α̂ > 1 is evidence, not proof, that exceedance is forced — which is precisely why it drives a calibrated screen rather than a bare threshold (below).

On known truth the α̂ estimate behaves as intended where the exceeded dimension resists the range degeneracy, and it exposes — rather than hides — where it does not. For a small multirotor exceeding its speed or climb envelope, α̂ rises monotonically with true exceedance (for a speed excess: 0.82 in-envelope, 1.00 at +20%, 1.76 at +100%), so the in-envelope control is separated from a 20% excess at recoverable geometry, and α̂ under-estimates the true exceedance in these tested cells (1.00 and 1.76 against true 1.2 and 2.0; similarly for climb) — an empirically conservative direction here, though an upper-bound optimizer estimate is not guaranteed to underestimate truth in general. For a fixed-wing speed dash, however, α̂ is essentially flat across scale (0.59 / 0.59 / 0.59 whether the truth is in-envelope or twice it) even though the signature is plainly separable in the sightlines (6.5° of clean separation at 2×): a nearer, slower in-envelope aircraft within the searched family reproduces the same bearings, so a horizontal speed excess is not forced by the geometry and cannot be attributed from bearings alone. This is the central lesson of the capability analysis — pairwise signature separability (the exceeded signature differs from its in-envelope counterpart) and forced envelope exceedance (no in-envelope trajectory reproduces the bearings) are distinct thresholds, and a candidate exceedance can be clearly separable yet formally unattributable. At weak geometry the small-platform speed and climb excesses fall below even the separability screen.

Status of the capability detector. The α̂ statistic is a validated measurement; converting it into a binary "exceedance forced" detection requires a calibrated threshold — because α̂ is an optimizer estimate of the family minimum α* (hence biased upward), the threshold must be set empirically from the null distribution of α̂ over in-envelope controls, not from the nominal unit scale (α̂ = 1). That calibration and a frozen held-out evaluation are future work; this paper reports the method, the separability screen, and the measurement's validated behavior, and makes no quantitative detection-rate claim. In the benchmark harness the binary claim is disabled by construction (a null result) until a calibration artifact, bound to the exact detector configuration, is supplied; the statistic is not wired into the interactive analysis tool.

5The benchmark as a defect-discovery instrument

Because truth is known, the benchmark doubles as a test harness for the analysis software itself. Round-1 findings led to changes now in the tool's source: live linear fits surface conditioning and collapse rather than publishing degenerate tracks silently, and an optimizer-stopping rule that had mislabeled a genuine balloon fit "incomplete" (its unresolved parameters were provably unobservable from the clip — flame-out beyond the clip end leaves sink parameters without effect) was reclassified as an identifiability statement. Round 2 confirmed, by independent replay against production code, two further issues, confirmed and documented for repair: a decisiveness gate in the minimum-acceleration method that vetoes correct geometric range on slow targets (its pure-smoothness optimum sat at truth — 1.5–1.8 NM against 1.73 — before a resolution-dependent secondary test forced the speed prior — at the 300 kt default the answer moved to 5.9 NM at a 0.003° residual, and a replay with the deployed 380 kt input returned 6.9 NM, worse), and a convergence failure in the fixed-wing fit (returned cost 13.7 versus 9.9 available in-bounds). We highlight the first because of its direct bearing wherever slow, close candidate explanations are weighed against fast, distant ones: on slow, close objects, this class of method can currently overstate range by several times while reporting an excellent fit, and the failure is invisible without ground truth.

6Guidance for analysts

  • Check geometry before interpreting anything. Compute the conditioning statistic — a linear-family diagnostic, sharper than intuition about subtended arc. A warning means any linear-family range should be treated as unreliable pending corroboration — not categorically rejected: the detector's false-positive rate on the held-out test partition was 45% at its balanced operating point (0% on the external sentinel pool), so it flags risk, and a clean reading proves nothing.
  • Never rank hypotheses by residual alone. The benchmark's clearest repeated result is fits that thread the sightlines beautifully at the wrong distance.
  • Use model panels, not single fits. Which model classes can cheaply explain the sightlines — and which cannot — carries more information than any one method's answer.
  • Treat filter tuning as noise-color dependent. Hand-tracked pointing error is plausibly autocorrelated (demonstrated here in simulation; calibration pending); run a process-noise sweep before trusting smoother output.
  • Prefer declared indeterminacy to confident artifacts. "These methods cannot determine range from this data" is a finding, and often the correct one: it was true of the unconstrained-method set for 59% of this benchmark's held-out scenarios.

7Limitations

All results are synthetic; external validity rests on the generators, which were built adversarially but not validated against instrumented truth. The operator-wobble model is behaviorally plausible, not calibrated to real tracking data (calibration from autotracked archival footage is planned). Terrain is a flat-datum proxy; the benchmark uses a single 70 m/s sensor speed — surveillance-class, not fast-jet, geometry. Optimizer budgets were not identical across methods: the balloon and multirotor fits ran a deterministic search at reduced budget and without production parameter seeding, while the fixed-wing and spline methods ran their production defaults — within-family comparisons are consistent, but cross-family speed/accuracy contrasts carry that asymmetry, and deployed (seeded, larger-budget) physics fits are likely modestly better than benchmarked here. The simulation also omits several real error sources entirely: platform navigation and boresight error, rolling-shutter and compression artifacts, and terrain-induced effects. The wind-agreement metric covers base-wind parameters only. Selection-rule results are audit-corrected re-evaluations, not preregistered, and their cross-geometry transfer limits are stated in §4.7. The capability detector (§4.8) is reported as a validated measurement only: its detection threshold is uncalibrated, so no detection-rate, false-alarm, or minimum-detectable-exceedance performance number is claimed. α̂ is an optimizer upper-bound estimate of a finite-dimensional search family's minimum, validated on clean truth for three dimensions of two catalogue airframes (multirotor speed/climb, fixed-wing speed) at three scales; the coherent novel-combination signatures are not in that validation table, and behavior under observation noise and across the full catalogue is untested here. The per-cell repeats share one deterministic truth (capability truth, orbit geometry, and clean observation do not depend on the seed), so they confirm reproducibility rather than sampling independent realizations.

8Reproducibility

The complete harness, scenario definitions, audit history, and analysis protocols are in the open-source repository (benchmarks/botbench/ in Sitrec). Scenario generation and all solver results are deterministic (identical inputs reproduce identical estimates and metrics; wall-clock timings naturally vary). Three commands regenerate the sweep results on commodity hardware: npm run bench-bot (~40 s), npm run bench-bot-classify (~45 s), npm run bench-bot-physics (~1 h), and — for the §4.8 capability separability screen and α̂ measurement — npm run bench-bot-capval (~6 min). The production-issue replay diagnostics quoted in §5 come from targeted single-scenario replays documented in the audit trail, not from these sweep commands. Sightline-to-scenario bridges allow any benchmark case to be loaded into the interactive tool for visual inspection; the tool's independent analysis of a bridged case matched the generated truth to the reported precision (3.0 NM slant range, 12.3 kt drift, against a 12.3 kt true wind).
