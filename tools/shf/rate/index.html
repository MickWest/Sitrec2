How this is calculated, and why the curve has this shape →

What the numbers mean
  • Each count is the flares one observer at this latitude could see in the whole sky above the horizon. It is not a worldwide total. The times are local solar time. The count is the average over the observer's longitude: the orbital planes drift against the Sun through the day, so the night at one longitude can differ from this average (in the simulation, three longitudes 120° apart differed by 6% of the count on a typical night and by up to 47%).
  • Visible flares at least double the satellite's normal brightness. All also counts faint glints at the edge of the 5° flare cone.
  • A flare needs the Sun about 28° to 47° below the horizon, so there are none in twilight. Flares are low in the sky, below about 15°, toward the direction of the Sun.
  • This is the expected (average) number. Real nights scatter about it by roughly the square root of the count. Sky conditions — the Moon, haze, light pollution — are not included.
  • The nights are real dates. The orbital planes of the 43°, 53° and 70° shells turn around the Earth at a few degrees a day, and on a given night a shell of a few planes (the 70° shells) can give half or twice the seasonal average. The model places every measured plane where it is on the night, so the curve is uneven from night to night, as the real count is. That ties the page to the years around the measurement: it covers , one calendar year at a time (the year control above the chart), and the table is rebuilt when the constellation is measured again.
  • The model reproduces a full simulation of the flares within about 1% in total, within 2% on a typical night, and within a few percent at any one latitude over the year. The synthetic constellation it uses is measured from the real element set, and matches the real count within 2% from 50°S to 60°N on the nights it was tested. The constellation changes with every launch, so the numbers drift as the measurement ages.
  • For flares at a real place and time, with directions, use the Flare Predictor. The model, where each part comes from, and its tests are on the model page.
