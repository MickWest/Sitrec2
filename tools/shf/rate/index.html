What the numbers mean
  • Each count is the flares one observer at this latitude could see in the whole sky above the horizon. It is not a worldwide total. The longitude does not matter, because the times are local solar time.
  • Visible flares at least double the satellite's normal brightness. All also counts faint glints at the edge of the 5° flare cone.
  • A flare needs the Sun about 28° to 47° below the horizon, so there are none in twilight. Flares are low in the sky, below about 15°, toward the direction of the Sun.
  • This is the expected (average) number. Real nights scatter about it by roughly the square root of the count. Sky conditions — the Moon, haze, light pollution — are not included.
  • The formula matches a full simulation of the synthetic Starlink constellation with R² 0.93 to 0.98 at most latitudes from 35°S to 65°N. It is least accurate near 55° in winter, where it can be up to twice too high. The synthetic constellation itself gives fewer flares than the real one above about 45°.
  • For flares at a real place and time, with directions, use the Flare Predictor. The formula, how it was found, and its tests are on the formula page.
