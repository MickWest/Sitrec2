How the flare rate is calculated

The flare rate page does not scan satellites. It uses a formula that comes from the geometry of a Starlink flare, with one constant fitted to a full simulation. This page gives the formula, where each part comes from, and why the number of flares through the year is not a simple curve.

1. The Sun must be 28° to 47° below the horizon

A Starlink satellite has a large flat antenna panel that faces straight down. When sunlight reflects from the panel toward you, you see a brief flash: a flare. The flare counts when the reflected ray comes within 5° of your eye (all flares), or within 4.44°, where the flash at least doubles the satellite's normal brightness (visible flares).

Because the panel faces down, it can reflect only sunlight that comes from below the satellite's horizontal. But the satellite must also be in sunlight, so that light must pass above the Earth's edge. Seen from the satellite, the Earth's edge is below the horizontal by an angle E = acos(R / (R + h)), where R is the Earth's radius (6,371 km) and h is the satellite's altitude: 18.9° at 360 km, 21.5° at 480 km and 23.6° at 580 km. So the sunlight always grazes past the Earth's edge, and the reflected ray comes down to an observer who sees the satellite low in the sky, in the direction of the Sun below the horizon.

Let D be how far the Sun is below your horizon, e the satellite's elevation, and θ the angle, at the Earth's centre, from you to the point under the satellite. In the plane of the drawing, the angle between the reflected ray and your eye is 2θ + e − D, with θ = 90° − e − asin(cos E × cos e). This gives a window of Sun depths for each shell:

  • Lower edge B. The angle is smallest at an elevation e* where sin e* = tan E / √3, about 13°. The glint is within the limit G only if D > 180° − e* − 2 × asin(cos E × cos e*) − G. When the Sun is less deep, no reflected ray comes close enough to your eye. That is why there are no flares in twilight.
  • Upper edge U = 2E. When the Sun is deeper, the satellites in the right place are in the Earth's shadow.

Inside the window the rate is a broad hump, lower at both edges. The formula uses W = √sin(180° × X), where X goes from 0 at the lower edge to 1 at the upper edge. This shape was chosen to fit the simulated flares; it is not derived. The same geometry also explains why flares are low in the sky (below about 15°) and in the direction of the Sun: in the simulation, 90% are within 5° of the Sun's azimuth.

2. Where the flaring satellites are

The satellites that flare for you are in a small zone about 15° to 20° away from you (measured at the Earth's centre), in the direction of the Sun. For a shell with edge dip E, that distance is C = 86° − asin(cos E × cos 4°). The latitude of the zone is

Z = asin(sin L × cos C + cos L × sin C × cos A)

where L is your latitude and A is the Sun's azimuth (its compass direction). So the zone is north of you when the Sun is toward the north, and south of you when it is toward the south. The Sun's direction at the times it is 28° to 47° down changes through the year, so the glint zone moves north and south with the seasons. At 35°N, for example, it is at about 31°–33°N in early January, and at 45°–55°N in late April.

3. Satellites crowd at their turning latitude

A satellite in an orbit tilted at inclination i goes north to latitude i, turns, and goes south to −i. Like a pendulum at the ends of its swing, it spends the most time near the turning points. So a shell of satellites is thinly spread over the equator and densely packed near ±i. The fraction of a shell south of latitude Y is F(Y) = ½ + asin(sin Y / sin i) / 180°, and the density per area is the change of F over a band, divided by the band's area. The formula uses a band of ±3°.

4. Why the curve is not simple

The flare count for a night is the product of two things that change through the year in different ways:

  1. How long the Sun stays 28° to 47° down. This depends on your latitude and the season. In summer at mid and high latitudes the Sun does not get deep enough, or only for a short time near midnight.
  2. How many satellites are in the glint zone. The zone moves north and south with the seasons (section 2), and the density of satellites there rises sharply each time the zone crosses a shell's turning latitude (section 3).

Each crossing makes a peak, and the Starlink shells have four turning latitudes, so one latitude can have several peaks a year. The pattern repeats on both sides of the June solstice, because the Sun's path does.

Examples from the formula:

  • 35°N. In January the zone is at 31°–33°N, and the 43° and 53° shells give about 400 flares a night together. In March and October the zone crosses 43°, and the 43° shells give 416 of about 780: the first bump. In late April and mid-August the zone is at 45°–55°N, over the 53° shells' turning latitude, and those shells give 1,195 of 1,352: the main peaks. On 21 June the Sun goes only 31.6° down, so only the lowest shells flare, for a short time near midnight: about 81.
  • 45°N. In December and January the zone is over the 43° turning latitude. In February and October it crosses 53°. From early May to mid-August the Sun never gets 28° down, so there are no flares at all.
  • The equator. The zone stays within about 10° of the equator, far from every turning latitude, and the Sun always goes deep. So the count is almost the same all year (about 230 to 270).

The same two effects shape each night. When the Sun goes deeper than a shell's window around midnight, those satellites are in the Earth's shadow, so the flares come in two groups, after dusk and before dawn. On a short summer night the Sun reaches the window only near midnight, so there is one group. The twelve shells are at different altitudes, so each has its own window, and together they soften the edges.

5. The formula

Angles are in degrees. Inputs: L = your latitude (north positive); N = day of the year (1 January = 0); T = local solar time in hours (noon is when the Sun is highest); R = 6,371 km; G = 4.44 (visible) or 5 (all); K = 0.3147 (visible) or 0.3519 (all).

The Sun

S = −23.44 × cos(360 × (N + 10) / 365.25)          solar declination
H = 15 × (T − 12)                                  hour angle
D = −asin(sin L × sin S + cos L × cos S × cos H)   depth below the horizon
A = atan2(−sin H × cos S,
          cos L × sin S − sin L × cos S × cos H)   azimuth

For each shell (inclination I, altitude h, n satellites)

E = acos(R / (R + h))                     dip of the Earth's edge
M = asin(tan E / √3)
U = 2 × E                                 upper edge of the window
B = 180 − M − 2 × asin(cos E × cos M) − G lower edge of the window
X = (D − B) / (U − B)
W = √sin(180 × X) if 0 < X < 1, else 0   the hump in the window
C = 86 − asin(cos E × cos 4)              distance to the glint zone
Z = asin(sin L × cos C + cos L × sin C × cos A)   latitude of the glint zone
F(Y) = 0.5 + asin(sin Y / sin I) / 180    (sin Y / sin I limited to −1 … +1)
Q = (F(Z+3) − F(Z−3)) / (2π × (sin(Z+3) − sin(Z−3)))   density at the zone

The rate

flares per hour = K × (n × Q × W, added over all shells)

Flares per night is this rate added up from noon to the next noon; the page uses 5-minute steps. K is the only fitted number. It was fitted to a simulation of every night of 2026 at seven latitudes.

6. How accurate it is

  • R² is the fraction of the night-to-night variation in the simulation that the formula explains (1 is a perfect match). Over the nights of the year it is 0.97 at 20°N, 0.91 at 35°N, 0.95 at 45°N, 0.97 at 65°N and 0.91 at 35°S. At five latitudes not used in the fit, it is 0.93 to 0.98, except 10°N, where the curve is almost flat.
  • The mean per night is usually within about 5% of the simulation.
  • It is least accurate near a turning latitude where the zone stays for a long time. At 55°N, R² is 0.50, and in December the formula gives about twice the simulated count.
  • It reproduces the simulation of the synthetic constellation, not the real sky. The synthetic constellation is within about 10% of the real one up to 35° latitude, but gives fewer flares further north: −19% at 45°N, −37% at 55°N and −65% at 65°N.
  • It is for an observer at sea level, and it does not include sky conditions such as the Moon, haze and light pollution. Real nights scatter about the expected number by roughly the square root of the count.
