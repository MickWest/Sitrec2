About Starlink Horizon Flares

What is a Starlink horizon flare?

A Starlink horizon flare is a brief bright point of light often brighter than Venus. They are seen low over the horizon well after the Sun has set. Each satellite carries a large, flat antenna that faces the ground. When that flat surface catches the Sun at just the right angle, it acts like a mirror and the satellite briefly becomes very bright.

Because the are tens of thousands of satellites in orbit, you get a constant, but random-looking, sequence of flares in the region of the sky above the sun. This is sometimes mistaken for a formation of lights, an "orb", or maneuvering UFOs. How it works in detail

In its operational orbit, each Starlink satellite flies with the flat underside of its bus — the phased-array antennas and mirrored panels — pointing straight down at the ground (nadir) to serve its users, with the solar panels extended above to catch the Sun. Because that reflective underside faces the ground, it lies flat against the curve of the Earth — tangential to the surface directly beneath the satellite, not tilted toward space. Smooth and flat, at narrow angles it behaves like a mirror: a specular reflector (a “glint”) that throws the Sun's light in one sharp, narrow beam instead of scattering it — which is what makes the flare so intense, orders of magnitude brighter than ordinary diffuse reflection.

You see a flare only when the geometry lines up exactly. Reflect your line of sight to the satellite off that downward-facing underside; if the reflected line points back at the Sun, the mirror is aimed at you and the satellite flare. That is precisely the test Sitrec performs — reflect the observer → satellite vector about the panel's nadir normal and measure the angle to the Sun; within a couple of degrees it flares, peaking when the alignment is near-perfect. The window is tiny and the satellite is moving fast, so each flare lasts only a few seconds — yet it can briefly outshine Venus.

Lighting an underside that faces the ground means the sunlight has to arrive almost horizontally, grazing in over the Earth's limb. That only happens when the satellite sits near the edge of the Earth as you see it — low on your horizon — and the reflected beam then comes down to you at a shallow angle. So the flares appear in a small patch of sky AARO calls the flare window: between the horizon and only about 10° above it, clustered toward the Sun's position below the horizon. For the nominal 550 km orbit this happens while the Sun sits roughly 38°–46° below the horizon. That is why they are called horizon flares, and why they show up over the spot where the Sun has set (or is about to rise).

And despite sometimes being confused with one, a horizon-flare display is not a moving train of lights. The familiar “Starlink train” is a separate effect: for a few days after launch the satellites fly in an “open-book” ascent configuration with their solar panels turned toward Earth, and it is sunlight off those solar panels that draws the line of bright dots. A horizon flare is the opposite — each flare is a different operational satellite, out of the thousands overhead in many orbital planes, catching the Sun for an instant off its mirrored underside as it crosses the flare window. The result is a random-looking sprinkle of flares popping in and out in one small patch of sky — which AARO notes can look like spinning lights, glowing orbs that vanish and reappear, or shapes such as triangles, and is a common source of UAP reports.
